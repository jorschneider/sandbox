How long does Science™

Here is a strange fact about modern life: nearly every week, a new study announces that some ordinary habit will add years to your life, or quietly subtract them. Coffee saves you. Sitting kills you. A daily glass of wine is either medicine or poison, depending on the news cycle. What nobody ever does is add it all up. So I built a machine that does. Choose a life below. The machine will throw every study in a blender, pour you a number, and take that number far more seriously than any honest scientist ever would.

    A note on the methodology, which is a crime

    Correlation is not a bank account.

    Let me be honest about what just happened. Every number in this machine comes from a real, frequently-cited observational study. And nearly every one of them measures the same thing — not what a habit does to you, but what kind of person tends to already have it. Daily flossers live longer, sure. They also tend to have dentists, salaries, and the sort of orderly life in which remembering a small nightly task is even possible. The floss isn't doing the work. The floss is a tell.

    This is the central confusion of the entire longevity genre, and it's worth saying plainly: you cannot make a deposit of green tea, a withdrawal of red meat, and check your balance in decades. The effects overlap, cancel, and mostly re-describe your zip code. That's why the machine hands you two numbers. The big glowing one adds every association as though the universe were a spreadsheet. The smaller one — with a straight face — does what a real epidemiologist does, which is assume your fourth healthy habit doesn't pay like your first, and that no one has ever become immortal by reading a press release.

    The gap between those two numbers is the whole point. It is the distance between what Science™ says and what science says. Mind the gap. Then go for a walk — not because a website told you to, but because it's nice out, and on this one narrow question the studies, for once, agree.
