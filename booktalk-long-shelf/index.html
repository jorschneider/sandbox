The long shelf

Forty-seven books, from 1872 to 2010, on eleven shelves. Each one gets at AI by way of something else: missile labs and machine shops, a Qing sorcery scare, a medieval stirrup and some monks.

A reading list for Jordan Schneider and Julian Gewirtz. Every pick comes with its reviews.

The map The shelves Double bills Near misses

So far

  1. No. 1 · Read The Social History of the Machine Gun John Ellis, 1975

    How one weapon remade war, empire and the officer class’s idea of itself.

  2. No. 2 · Up next Men, Machines, and Modern Times Elting E. Morison, 1966

    Essays on how institutions fight even a plainly better machine, starting with the Navy and continuous-aim gunfire.

The shelves below keep pulling on threads from those two: who designs a technology and for whom, how states race for it, how institutions absorb or refuse it, and what it does to work, trust and safety.

House rules

  1. Published in 2010 or earlier.
  2. Great on its own terms. Every card links a prize, serious reviews or its field’s verdict. Famous books whose central claim is disputed carry a Provocation tag.
  3. Mostly not about AI. The handful that are (Turkle, Penrose, The Fifth Generation and a few novels) argued about it before it worked.
  4. Enough story and argument to fill an hour.

01The Race

Sputnik moments, national champions, bubbles and the politics of catching up. 5 books.

No. 01History · 1983

Networks of Power

Electrification in Western Society, 1880–1930

Thomas P. Hughes

How Edison, Insull and their counterparts in Berlin and London built electric power systems, and why each country’s came out different.

The angleAI is turning into a power-grid story. Hughes’s “reverse salients,” the laggard parts that hold a whole system back, are the right tool for chips, data centers and transmission lines.

Episode questionWhat is AI’s reverse salient today: chips, power, data or people?

ReceptionWon the Society for the History of Technology’s Dexter Prize (Johns Hopkins University Press, prize list).

No. 02Polemic · 1983Provocation

The Fifth Generation

Artificial Intelligence and Japan’s Computer Challenge to the World

Edward A. Feigenbaum and Pamela McCorduck

Two AI insiders warn that Japan’s state-backed Fifth Generation project will seize the lead in “knowledge processing” unless America answers.

The angleThe last time America panicked about an Asian AI program. The program fizzled; the panic still moved money in Washington.

Episode questionWhat did the 1980s panic get wrong about Japan that today’s might be getting wrong about China?

ReceptionThe Christian Science Monitor found it “highly useful for anyone interested in this crucial race.” Joseph Weizenbaum took it apart in The New York Review of Books, and John McCarthy noted that nobody at ICOT was working on common sense.

No. 03History · 1985Start here

…the Heavens and the Earth

A Political History of the Space Age

Walter A. McDougall

How Sputnik turned American and Soviet leaders into technocrats, and the space race into a contest over which system could command technological change.

The angleThe template for every “Sputnik moment,” including the one declared when DeepSeek shipped. McDougall is skeptical of what crash programs do to the states that run them.

Episode questionDid the Sputnik panic make America stronger, or just more like the rival it feared?

ReceptionWon the 1986 Pulitzer Prize for History and the Society for the History of Technology’s Dexter Prize. NASA historian Roger Launius calls it a “classic study in the arena of space policy history” (summary of its reception).

No. 04Political economy · 1994

“Rich Nation, Strong Army”

National Security and the Technological Transformation of Japan

Richard J. Samuels

Japan’s long campaign, from the Meiji arsenals to the 1990s, to absorb foreign technology, spread it through industry and nurture its own producers, all in the name of security.

The angleA rulebook for techno-nationalism written a generation before Beijing’s version: what it takes to make a borrowed technology your own.

Episode questionIs China’s AI strategy Meiji Japan’s playbook with more compute?

ReceptionWon the Association for Asian Studies’ 1996 John Whitney Hall Book Prize and the Arisawa Memorial Prize; the Hall citation called it “a book with many lessons” (MIT News).

No. 05History · 2006Start here

The Shock of the Old

Technology and Global History since 1900

David Edgerton

A history of technology in use rather than invention: horses in Hitler’s army, corrugated iron, the rickshaw and the long afterlife of “old” machines.

The angleThe corrective to invention-centered AI talk. What matters is who uses a technology and how widely, not who demos it first.

Episode questionIf Edgerton wrote about AI in 2026, what would be his horses and corrugated iron?

ReceptionThe Sunday Times said Edgerton “eviscerates our obsession with novelty,” the Times called it iconoclastic, and Simon Jenkins wrote in the Guardian that it was a book he could use (Profile Books).

02The Lab

How builders actually build: teams, managers, deadlines, and the inventions that got away. 3 books.

No. 06History · 1980

The Origins of the Turbojet Revolution

Edward W. Constant II

Why the jet engine came from outsiders in Britain and Germany: engineers who saw, years ahead, that the piston engine and propeller would hit a wall.

The angleConstant’s “presumptive anomaly,” the prediction that a dominant technology will fail before it actually does, is the best frame for arguing about whether scaling will hit a wall.

Episode questionWho sees a technology’s limits first, and does deep learning have a presumptive anomaly?

ReceptionWon the Society for the History of Technology’s Dexter Prize in 1981 (prize list).

No. 07Reportage · 1981

The Soul of a New Machine

Tracy Kidder

Kidder embeds with a Data General team racing to build a 32-bit minicomputer, and watches how engineers are driven, managed and burned out.

The angleStill the best book about life on a frontier team, written about a machine nobody remembers. “Signing up” and the pinball theory of reward (win, and you get to play again) are alive in every lab.

Episode questionWhy do brilliant people sign up for brutal crunches, and who captures the value?

ReceptionWon the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction and the 1982 National Book Award; the New York Times said Kidder raised his subject “to a high level of narrative art” (Wikipedia).

No. 08History · 1997

Crystal Fire

The Birth of the Information Age

Michael Riordan and Lillian Hoddeson

The invention of the transistor at Bell Labs in 1947, and the rivalries among Shockley, Bardeen and Brattain behind it.

The angleEvery chip in today’s export-control fight descends from this lab bench. It is also a portrait of William Shockley, a brilliant and impossible manager.

Episode questionWhat made Bell Labs work, and could an AI lab copy it?

ReceptionWon the Society for the History of Technology’s first Sally Hacker Prize in 1999 and became the basis for the PBS documentary Transistorized! (Wikipedia).

03The War Room

Command, conquest and the weapons that changed who wins, from the stirrup to the Cold War’s closed world. 8 books.

No. 09History · 1962Provocation

Medieval Technology and Social Change

Lynn White Jr.

Three essays arguing that the stirrup, the heavy plough and new sources of power remade medieval Europe, most famously that the stirrup gave rise to feudalism.

The angleThe boldest case for one small device reordering a whole society. Read it with its critics to ask how seriously to take claims that AI alone will remake armies and states.

Episode questionIf AI is the new stirrup, who become the new knights?

ReceptionWon the History of Science Society’s 1963 Pfizer Award, and Joseph Needham called it “the most stimulating book of the century on the history of technology” (Wikipedia). The stirrup thesis has drawn heavy fire ever since (Wikipedia).

No. 10Psychology · 1976Start here

On the Psychology of Military Incompetence

Norman F. Dixon

A psychologist works through a century of British military disasters, from the Crimea to the fall of Singapore, and argues they came from the kind of person armies promote.

The angleBig institutions reward people who follow rules, flatter superiors and dread looking foolish, then hand them the new technology. Read it to ask who ends up in charge of AI adoption at the Pentagon, or in the PLA.

Episode questionDo militaries select for exactly the leaders worst placed to use AI well?

ReceptionHas drawn favourable reviews for over 40 years, is still used in leadership studies, and in 2015 topped BookFinder’s list of the most sought out-of-print books in the US (Wikipedia).

No. 11History · 1981Start here

The Tools of Empire

Technology and European Imperialism in the Nineteenth Century

Daniel R. Headrick

How steamboats, quinine and breech-loading guns let Europe conquer much of Africa and Asia cheaply in the nineteenth century.

The angleThe sequel to the machine-gun episode: a technology gap so wide that conquest became cheap. Read it to ask whether an AI gap between countries could ever open that far.

Episode questionCould a lead in frontier AI ever be as decisive as quinine and the steamboat were?

ReceptionReviewed across business, economic, African and Asian history journals, including the Journal of African History and Business History Review.

No. 12History · 1982

The Pursuit of Power

Technology, Armed Force, and Society since A.D. 1000

William H. McNeill

A thousand years of how markets, states and weapons shaped each other, starting with the commercial boom of eleventh-century China.

The angleMcNeill’s case that war became a market business, with states buying what merchants and inventors could sell. It frames the fight over whether labs or governments set the pace of military AI.

Episode questionIs military AI being driven by states or by markets, and does the answer change who wins?

ReceptionReviewed for the New York Times by Stanley Hoffmann (Wikipedia); its publisher still sells it as a “magnificent synthesis of military, technological, and social history” (University of Chicago Press).

No. 13History · 1985

Command in War

Martin van Creveld

Command from antiquity to Vietnam as a problem of information: how commanders gathered it, filtered it and coped with never having enough.

The angleEvery promise that AI will lift the fog of war has precedents here. Van Creveld’s answer was never more data; it was command organized to live with uncertainty.

Episode questionDoes AI shrink the fog of war, or just move it?

ReceptionIn the Naval Institute’s Proceedings, Michael Palmer called it excellent, original and significant (USNI); Brian Bond found it “absorbing reading for military historians” in International Security (Project MUSE).

No. 14Sociology · 1990

Inventing Accuracy

A Historical Sociology of Nuclear Missile Guidance

Donald MacKenzie

How American missiles got more accurate: less through the march of physics than through lab rivalries, service politics and fights over what “accuracy” meant.

The angleCapabilities don’t simply arrive; labs, militaries and politics decide what counts. A natural companion to the fights over AI benchmarks.

Episode questionWho decides what counts as an AI capability, and what happens when the measure becomes the target?

ReceptionWon the Society for Social Studies of Science’s Ludwik Fleck Prize, its annual book award.

No. 15Political science · 1991

Winning the Next War

Innovation and the Modern Military

Stephen Peter Rosen

How twentieth-century militaries really adopted new ways of fighting, from carrier aviation to helicopters: through new career paths for young officers more than budgets or defeats.

The angleThe sequel to Morison’s gunfire story. If you want to know whether the Pentagon will truly use AI, Rosen says to watch the promotion boards.

Episode questionWho in today’s military gets promoted for mastering AI, and who gets passed over?

ReceptionWon the 1992 Edgar S. Furniss Book Award for an outstanding contribution to national security studies (Wikipedia).

No. 16History · 1996

The Closed World

Computers and the Politics of Discourse in Cold War America

Paul N. Edwards

How Cold War strategy shaped computing, from the SAGE air-defense network to the military money behind early AI and cognitive science.

The angleAI grew up on Pentagon funding and a dream of total command and control. Edwards shows the machines and the metaphors, the mind as computer, developing together.

Episode questionHow much of AI’s DNA is still Cold War command and control?

ReceptionHoward Rheingold called it “one of the most important books of the 20th century” (Powell’s).

04The State

Planners, cyberneticians and an old argument over whether self-interest can tame power. 3 books.

No. 17Intellectual history · 1977

The Passions and the Interests

Political Arguments for Capitalism before Its Triumph

Albert O. Hirschman

How seventeenth- and eighteenth-century thinkers came to argue that the steady pursuit of gain could tame more dangerous passions, like ambition and the lust for power.

The angleThe original alignment argument: make a powerful drive safe by channelling it into a predictable one. Read it to ask whether tying AI to commercial interest makes it tamer or just stronger.

Episode questionIs profit a safe harness for AI, the way Montesquieu hoped commerce would be for princes?

ReceptionPrinceton keeps it in print as a classic, with a foreword by Amartya Sen; reviewers called it “a fresh and exciting argument of a fascinating thesis” (Princeton University Press).

No. 18History · 2002Start here

From Newspeak to Cyberspeak

A History of Soviet Cybernetics

Slava Gerovitch

How cybernetics went from a “bourgeois pseudoscience” in Stalin’s USSR to the language Soviet scientists hoped would rationalize the planned economy.

The angleThe history behind Red Plenty: scientists who believed computers would make the plan work, and a party that let them talk but not decide.

Episode questionCan a one-party state let AI reform it, or only borrow its vocabulary?

ReceptionHonourable mention for the 2003 Vucinich Book Prize; David Holloway called it “by far the best-informed and most insightful account of cybernetics in the Soviet Union” (author’s page).

No. 19Novel and history · 2010

Red Plenty

Francis Spufford

The Soviet dream of outproducing capitalism through mathematics, told in linked stories about Kantorovich, Khrushchev and the economists, engineers and fixers of the planned economy.

The angleThe original dream of running an economy by computer. With AI planning agents back in vogue, it reads as both temptation and warning.

Episode questionCould AI do what Gosplan couldn’t, or is the problem knowledge rather than computing power?

ReceptionAdam Roberts in Strange Horizons called it “a brilliant intervention into utopian discourses,” and it was listed for the Orwell Prize, the Ondaatje Prize and the BSFA Award (Wikipedia).

05The Shop Floor

Who does the work, who loses the skill, and who decides. 9 books.

No. 20Philosophy · 1958

The Human Condition

Hannah Arendt

Arendt separates labor, work and action, and asks what a society loses when everything becomes producing and consuming.

The angleThe prologue opens with Sputnik and warns that automation may leave us “a society of laborers without labor.” Few books ask the post-work question as hard.

Episode questionIf AI does the labor, what is left for us to do, and will we know how to do it?

ReceptionReviewing it in Encounter in 1959, W. H. Auden confessed to a “jealous possessiveness” toward the book (Hannah Arendt Center).

No. 21History · 1963

The Making of the English Working Class

E. P. Thompson

How English workers made themselves a class between 1780 and 1832, including the Luddites, whom Thompson rescues from “the enormous condescension of posterity.”

The angleThe Luddites were fighting what machines were being used to do to them, more than the machines themselves. Read it before calling anyone an AI Luddite.

Episode questionWere the Luddites wrong, and who are today’s?

ReceptionThe Modern Library ranked it thirtieth among the hundred best nonfiction books of the twentieth century, and Sidney Pollard called it “a landmark in English historiography” (Wikipedia).

No. 22Oral history · 1974

Working

People Talk About What They Do All Day and How They Feel About What They Do

Studs Terkel

More than a hundred Americans, from a steelworker and a waitress to an executive, describe their jobs and what the jobs mean to them.

The angleBefore arguing about which jobs AI will take, hear people describe them. Terkel catches the pride, boredom and dignity that productivity numbers miss.

Episode questionWhich of Terkel’s jobs would AI improve, and which would it hollow out?

ReceptionA bestseller when it appeared, it became a Broadway musical and inspired a 2023 Netflix series hosted by Barack Obama (Wikipedia).

No. 23Sociology · 1974Start here

Labor and Monopoly Capital

The Degradation of Work in the Twentieth Century

Harry Braverman

A former coppersmith argues that management uses each new machine to strip skill out of jobs and move know-how from workers to managers.

The angleThe deskilling thesis that Noble and Zuboff argue with. Read it to ask whether AI deskills office work or moves expertise to whoever owns the model.

Episode questionDoes AI take the skill out of white-collar jobs, or put it back in?

ReceptionStarted what became known as the labor process debate, has been called one of the most important sociological books of its era, and topped the Wall Street Journal’s 2009 list of the five best books on working (Wikipedia).

No. 24History · 1983

More Work for Mother

The Ironies of Household Technology from the Open Hearth to the Microwave

Ruth Schwartz Cowan

Why labor-saving household machines didn’t save housewives time: standards rose with every new appliance.

The angleThe best history of a labor-saving tool that raised expectations instead of cutting hours. Watch for the same pattern in office AI.

Episode questionWill AI give knowledge workers more time, or more work?

ReceptionWon the Society for the History of Technology’s Dexter Prize in 1984. Early reviews were mixed, but it has since become a standard source in women’s studies and the history of technology (Ruth Schwartz Cowan).

No. 25History · 1984Start here

Forces of Production

A Social History of Industrial Automation

David F. Noble

How American industry chose numerically controlled machine tools over a rival technology that would have kept skilled machinists in charge.

The angleAutomation is a choice about who holds control. Noble shows managers and the Air Force picking the design that took knowledge off the shop floor.

Episode questionWho is choosing which kind of AI automation gets built, and for whose benefit?

ReceptionJames Fallows called it a “prodigious accomplishment” in The New York Review of Books, and Carroll Pursell predicted in the American Historical Review that it would become a classic in its field. David Brody attacked its dogmatism in Science; SHOT’s memorial to Noble lays out the fight.

No. 26History · 1984

From the American System to Mass Production, 1800–1932

The Development of Manufacturing Technology in the United States

David A. Hounshell

How American factories got from interchangeable parts at the federal armories to Ford’s moving assembly line, and how much slower and harder that was than the legend says.

The angleInterchangeable parts took decades and federal money before they paid for themselves. A sober guide to how long a general-purpose technology takes to reach the factory floor.

Episode questionIs AI still at the armory stage, or already at Highland Park?

ReceptionWon the Society for the History of Technology’s 1987 Dexter Prize (Wikipedia).

No. 27Sociology · 1988

In the Age of the Smart Machine

The Future of Work and Power

Shoshana Zuboff

Years of fieldwork in pulp mills and offices as computers arrived, and the managers and workers who fought over what the machines would be for.

The angleZuboff’s distinction is the one to borrow: computers can automate work, or “informate” it by giving workers new knowledge. Which one AI does is a management choice.

Episode questionWill companies use AI to automate their workers or to informate them?

ReceptionMerle Rubin in the Christian Science Monitor found it thorough and thoughtful, “lucid, even passionate at times” (review).

No. 28History · 2005

When Computers Were Human

David Alan Grier

Three centuries of human computers, many of them women, who calculated comet orbits, navigation and artillery tables by hand until machines took the job and the name.

The angleThe last job called “computer” was automated away. Grier shows how the work was organized, who did it and what became of them.

Episode questionWhat happened to the human computers, and what does it predict for knowledge workers now?

ReceptionGeorge Dyson called it an “authoritative, engaging, and richly detailed account,” and the IEEE Annals of the History of Computing recommended it to every historian of computing (Princeton University Press). It won a 2006 Independent Publisher Book Award.

06The Archive

Memory, writing, print and information overload: earlier moments when a new medium remade memory and trust. 4 books.

No. 29History · 1979

The Printing Press as an Agent of Change

Elizabeth L. Eisenstein

How print transformed European thought by standardizing, preserving and multiplying texts, and helped make the Reformation and the Scientific Revolution possible.

The angleEveryone calls AI the new printing press; this is the book on what the press actually did. The abridged Printing Revolution in Early Modern Europe is the podcast-length version.

Episode questionIs AI an agent of change the way print was, or just a faster copier?

ReceptionPraised for bringing historical rigor to McLuhan’s hunches about print, and still contested: in 2002 the American Historical Review staged a debate between Eisenstein and her critic Adrian Johns (Wikipedia).

No. 30Media theory · 1982

Orality and Literacy

The Technologizing of the Word

Walter J. Ong

How writing and print restructured human thought, and how cultures without writing think, remember and argue.

The angleOng’s “secondary orality,” a new oral culture built on electronic media, fits the chatbot: knowledge delivered as conversation rather than text.

Episode questionAre chatbots taking us back toward an oral culture?

ReceptionOng’s most widely known work, translated into eleven languages (Wikipedia).

No. 31Sociology · 1986

The Control Revolution

Technological and Economic Origins of the Information Society

James R. Beniger

The information society didn’t start with computers. It began in the nineteenth century, when railroads, factories and markets moved faster than anyone could control them.

The angleAI as the latest answer to a crisis of control: information technology arrives when the flow of goods and data outruns the people managing it.

Episode questionIs AI a new revolution, or the latest chapter of Beniger’s?

ReceptionWon the Association of American Publishers’ 1986 award for the most outstanding book in the social and behavioral sciences, and in 2007 the International Communication Association’s Fellows Book Award for standing the test of time (USC Annenberg). Cosma Shalizi called it “undoubtedly the best work ever done by a professor of communications” (The Bactra Review).

No. 32History · 2010

Too Much to Know

Managing Scholarly Information before the Modern Age

Ann M. Blair

How Renaissance scholars coped with a flood of printed books, using anthologies, encyclopedias, indexes and note-taking systems that literally cut and pasted text.

The angleInformation overload and the tools to tame it, centuries before search and summarization, along with the old complaint that shortcuts make readers lazy.

Episode questionIs AI summarization the florilegium of our age, and is the panic the same panic?

ReceptionJames Delbourgo, in Times Higher Education, found it timely and erudite (review).

07The Accident

Missed warnings, and the risks modern societies make for themselves. 2 books.

No. 33History · 1962

Pearl Harbor

Warning and Decision

Roberta Wohlstetter

Why America was surprised at Pearl Harbor even though its codebreakers were reading Japanese traffic: the signals were buried in noise.

The angleThe classic case that more information doesn’t mean better warning. Required for anyone who thinks AI will end intelligence failures.

Episode questionWould an AI analyst have caught the signal, or drowned in the noise faster?

ReceptionWon the 1963 Bancroft Prize; the military historian Eugene Rasor called it “the best and most comprehensive study” of the failure (Wikipedia).

No. 34Sociology · 1986

Risk Society

Towards a New Modernity

Ulrich Beck

Beck argues that modern societies are increasingly organized around risks they manufacture themselves, often invisible to ordinary people and defined by experts.

The angleAI risk is a textbook manufactured risk: global, hard to see and fought over by experts. Beck predicted the politics, including the collapse of trust when experts disagree.

Episode questionWho gets to define AI’s risks, and what happens to trust when the experts split?

ReceptionPublished in German just after Chernobyl, it became a highly influential, catalytic work of social theory (Wikipedia).

08The Mind

Machines that seem to think, and whether we can ever check their reasoning. 3 books.

No. 35Sociology · 1984

The Second Self

Computers and the Human Spirit

Sherry Turkle

An MIT sociologist’s interviews with children, hackers and early PC owners about how computers changed the way they thought about minds, life and themselves.

The angleChildren arguing over whether a computer toy is alive are the ancestors of today’s AI-companion users. Turkle watched people bond with machines decades before chatbots.

Episode questionWhat do our relationships with chatbots say about us?

ReceptionChristopher Lehmann-Haupt in the New York Times called it “a brilliant and challenging discussion presented with extraordinary clarity” (Penguin Random House).

No. 36Popular science · 1989Provocation

The Emperor’s New Mind

Concerning Computers, Minds, and the Laws of Physics

Roger Penrose

A great mathematical physicist argues that human understanding isn’t algorithmic, so no digital computer can think as we do, and that consciousness depends on physics we don’t yet understand.

The angleThe most serious argument from physics that machines can’t truly understand. Most AI researchers think it’s wrong; working out exactly why is the episode.

Episode questionIf Penrose is right, what are today’s models doing instead of thinking?

ReceptionWon the 1990 Science Book Prize, while philosophers, computer scientists and roboticists have attacked its thesis as mistaken (Wikipedia).

No. 37Sociology of science · 2001

Mechanizing Proof

Computing, Risk, and Trust

Donald MacKenzie

The long effort to prove software and chips correct, and the fights over what counts as a proof once a machine does the checking.

The angleFrom the author of Inventing Accuracy, the question under every AI safety plan: when can you trust a machine’s reasoning, and who checks the checker?

Episode questionWill anyone ever prove an AI system safe, or will we only come to trust it?

ReceptionBrian Hayes in American Scientist called it a “well-informed, nontechnical description” with “pleasantly informal” prose (American Scientist).

09China & the Long View

Sorcerers, bomb builders and the Western machines once held up as the measure of civilization. 3 books.

No. 38History · 1988

China Builds the Bomb

John Wilson Lewis and Xue Litai

How China built and tested its first atomic bomb in 1964 after the Soviets pulled their help: mass mobilization, a scientific elite and political will.

The angleThe precedent for building capability under technology denial. Read it with chip export controls in mind.

Episode questionDo export controls slow a determined state, or speed its self-reliance?

ReceptionPublishers Weekly called it “this important work of scholarship” (review).

No. 39History · 1989

Machines as the Measure of Men

Science, Technology, and Ideologies of Western Dominance

Michael Adas

How Europeans came to judge other civilizations, China and India above all, by their machines and science.

The angleTechnology as a yardstick of civilization: a lead in machines hardened into an ideology of superiority. AI is becoming the new yardstick.

Episode questionWill AI capability become the new measure of nations, and who gets ranked below?

ReceptionWon the Society for the History of Technology’s Dexter Prize in 1991; Alan Charles Kors reviewed it in The New York Times (Michael Adas).

No. 40History · 1990Start here

Soulstealers

The Chinese Sorcery Scare of 1768

Philip A. Kuhn

A panic over sorcerers said to steal souls by clipping men’s queues, and how the Qianlong emperor used it to discipline his own bureaucracy.

The angleA moral panic over an invisible threat, and an autocrat who turned it into a tool of control. Read it against today’s fears of deepfakes and AI subversion.

Episode questionWho benefits from a panic over an invisible, technical threat?

ReceptionWon the Association for Asian Studies’ Joseph Levenson Prize, and its Chinese translation sold more than 100,000 copies (Wikipedia).

10Novels & Stories

Fiction that saw it coming, or saw around it. 4 books.

No. 41Satire · 1872

Erewhon

Samuel Butler

A traveler finds a country that destroyed its machines centuries earlier, afraid they would evolve consciousness and enslave their makers.

The angleIts “Book of the Machines” chapters make one of the earliest arguments that machines could evolve past us. The Erewhonians did what today’s pause advocates only propose.

Episode questionIs Erewhon a satire of machine-smashers, or a warning that they had a point?

ReceptionGeorge Orwell praised the “imagination of a very high order” behind it, and Alan Turing cited its warning (Erewhon).

No. 42Novel · 1952

Player Piano

Kurt Vonnegut

In a future America run by engineers and managers, automated factories have made most workers useless, and a plant manager drifts toward rebels who want to smash the machines.

The angleThe first great novel of automation anxiety, drawn from Vonnegut’s years at General Electric. Its question is not whether the machines work, but what people are for once they do.

Episode questionWhat are people for once the machines work?

ReceptionGroff Conklin called it “a biting, vividly alive and very effectively understated anti-Utopia,” and it was nominated for the 1953 International Fantasy Award (Wikipedia).

No. 43Novel · 1959

A Canticle for Leibowitz

Walter M. Miller Jr.

After a nuclear war, monks spend centuries copying the surviving scraps of science, including a blueprint they revere without understanding, until civilization rebuilds and repeats its mistakes.

The angleFaithfully copying what you don’t understand, for centuries: a strange mirror for language models, and a novel about whether knowledge saves us.

Episode questionAre the monks of Leibowitz the first language models?

ReceptionWon the 1961 Hugo Award, and Time later called it “an extraordinary novel” (Wikipedia).

No. 44Stories · 1962

Labyrinths

Selected Stories and Other Writings

Jorge Luis Borges

Stories of an infinite library, a man who forgets nothing, forking paths and a writer who recomposes Don Quixote word for word.

The angle“The Library of Babel” is a model’s possibility space, “Funes the Memorious” is overfitting, and “Pierre Menard” is the authorship question.

Episode questionIs a language model the Library of Babel with a search box?

ReceptionPublished soon after Borges shared the Prix Formentor with Samuel Beckett; in 2008 the Society of Authors named it one of fifty outstanding translations of the previous half-century (Wikipedia).

11Lives

Biographies and memoirs of the people who built, funded and fretted over the machines. 3 books.

No. 45Memoir · 1918

The Education of Henry Adams

Henry Adams

A Boston patrician’s account of how his education failed to prepare him for a world remade by technology, including the chapter “The Dynamo and the Virgin.”

The angleBefore the dynamos at the 1900 Paris Exposition, Adams felt a new force he couldn’t understand. The founding memoir of technological vertigo.

Episode questionWhat is our dynamo, and what would Adams make of it?

ReceptionWon the 1919 Pulitzer Prize, and the Modern Library ranked it first among the 100 best English-language nonfiction books of the twentieth century (The Education of Henry Adams).

No. 46Double biography · 1980

John von Neumann and Norbert Wiener

From Mathematics to the Technologies of Life and Death

Steve J. Heims

Two prodigies who founded the computer age and split over military power: von Neumann embraced it, and Wiener refused weapons work after 1945.

The angleThe two paths open to every AI researcher today, made concrete.

Episode questionVon Neumann or Wiener: which would you be at a frontier lab?

ReceptionPhysics Today’s reviewer, Robert Andersen, rated it “highly recommended,” while faulting its prose (Physics Today, 1981).

No. 47Biography · 2001

The Dream Machine

J. C. R. Licklider and the Revolution That Made Computing Personal

M. Mitchell Waldrop

Licklider, a psychologist turned Pentagon program manager, funded the people who built time-sharing, networking and personal computing.

The angleHis 1960 essay “Man-Computer Symbiosis” is the copilot vision, sixty years early. And he shows how much one visionary funder can do.

Episode questionWho is today’s Licklider, and could a government program manager still matter that much?

ReceptionKirkus called it “a rollicking account of a good, old-fashioned visionary,” and Stripe Press brought it back into print in 2018 (2018 edition).

Double bills

Pairings that would make a two-part episode, or one good argument.

  • Two Sputnik moments

    …the Heavens and the Earth+The Fifth Generation

    Sputnik built NASA. The 1983 panic over Japan’s AI program helped launch DARPA’s Strategic Computing Initiative (Roland and Shiman’s Strategic Computing tells that story). Which one was DeepSeek?

  • The GE episode

    Forces of Production+Player Piano

    Vonnegut wrote his novel after years at General Electric watching machine tools get automated; Noble’s history includes a long fight over the same kind of machines inside a GE jet-engine plant.

  • Who keeps the skill

    Labor and Monopoly Capital+Forces of Production+In the Age of the Smart Machine

    Braverman says machines strip skill from work, Noble shows managers choosing the machines that do it, and Zuboff finds computers that could go either way.
