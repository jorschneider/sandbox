Franzefuss

Also called Tatteln; in Denmark, Frantsfuus-Spillet. Two players, 32 cards, first to 501.

The pack

Sevens up to aces, 32 cards. One suit is trumps, fixed by the card turned up at the deal.

Only card points count, never the number of tricks:

That is 152 in the pack, plus 10 for the last trick — 162 a deal.

The deal

Nine cards each, dealt alternately, non-dealer first. The next card is turned for trumps and the remaining thirteen form the stock.

Melds

Before the first card is led, each player may declare or pass. The better declaration scores — and scores every combination its owner holds. Declaring shows your hand's best meld to your opponent, so a thin one is worth hiding.

Runs use the natural order A K Q J 10 9 8 7. Ties go on the higher top card, then trumps, then to the non-dealer.

Play

Non-dealer leads. Highest card of the suit led takes the trick, unless it is trumped. The winner leads next.

While the stock lasts you may play anything you like — no need to follow suit. After each trick the winner draws first, then the loser; the last draw of all is the turned-up trump card.

Once the stock is gone — the last nine tricks — you must follow suit, you must beat a trump lead if you can, and you must trump when you are void.

Robbing

Holding the seven of trumps, you may swap it for the turned-up trump card on your turn, any time the turn-up is still there.

Scoring

Count the card points in your tricks, add the ten for the last trick, add your melds. Take none of the last nine tricks and you pay for the whole round: your opponent takes all 162.

First past 501 wins.

The coach

You do not have to read any of this first. Play a hand and the coach explains each rule at the moment it first decides something — what the cards are worth, why trumps rank strangely, when following suit starts to matter. Each lesson appears once. Tapping a dimmed card always tells you why it cannot be played.

A note on sources

Franzefuss was recorded in Hamburg in 1801 and printed in Austria in 1829, and the surviving descriptions are thin in places. The skeleton here — ranking, card values, the nine-card deal, the turn-up, robbing with the seven, drawing after each trick, following suit only once the stock is out, ten for the last trick, and the penalty for taking none of the last nine — follows those sources. Where they go quiet, the gaps are filled from its close relative Klaberjass: meld values, the declare-or-pass contest, and the 501 target.
