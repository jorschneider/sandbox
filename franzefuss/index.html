Franzefuß

Also Tatteln, Törteln, Därde; in Denmark Frantsfuus-Spillet. Two players, 32 cards, a partie to 100.

The pack

Sevens up to aces, 32 cards. One suit is trumps, fixed by the card turned up at the deal.

Trumps rankJ · 9 · A · 10 · K · Q · 8 · 7
Other suitsA · 10 · K · Q · J · 9 · 8 · 7

Only card points count, never the number of tricks:

Trump J20Trump 914
Ace11Ten10
King4Queen3
Jack29 · 8 · 70

That is 152 in the pack, plus 10 for the last trick — 162 a deal.

The deal

Nine cards each, dealt alternately, non-dealer first. The next card is turned for trumps and the remaining thirteen form the stock.

Announcing

Whenever it is your turn to lead, you may first announce a combination from your hand. Your opponent looks at their own hand and says good or not good — if theirs is better you score nothing of that class; if yours is better you score every combination of that class you hold. Sequences and sets are judged separately. Equal beats nothing: a tie scores for neither.

A run pays for every shorter run inside it, which is where the game gets its name:

Tattelrun of 3 in a suit3
Quartrun of 4 — and the two Tatteln in it10
Fußrun of 5 — three Tatteln, two Quarts32
Three of a kindtens and above only3
Four of a kindbeats three whatever the rank14

Runs use the natural order A K Q J 10 9 8 7 — the ten takes its natural place. Because a growing run keeps paying, a Tattel you announce now and extend later scores again: announce it once more when the card arrives.

Announcing tells your opponent what you are holding. That is the whole tension of it.

Play

Non-dealer leads. Highest card of the suit led takes the trick, unless it is trumped. The winner leads next.

While the stock lasts you may play anything you like — no need to follow suit. After each trick the winner draws first, then the loser; the last draw of all is the turned-up trump card.

Once the stock is gone — the last nine tricks — you must follow suit, you must beat a trump lead if you can, and you must trump when you are void.

Robbing

Holding the seven of trumps, you may swap it for the turned-up trump card on your turn, any time the turn-up is still there.

Scoring

Count the card points in your tricks, add the ten for the last trick, add whatever you announced. Take none of the last nine tricks and you pay for the whole round: your opponent takes all 162.

A partie is played to 100, as in Piquet. Deals are large, so a partie is short — historically the round was the unit of stake.

The coach

You do not have to read any of this first. Play a hand and the coach explains each rule at the moment it first decides something — what the cards are worth, why trumps rank strangely, when following suit starts to matter. Each lesson appears once. Tapping a dimmed card always tells you why it cannot be played.

Where these rules come from

Franzefuß was first recorded in 1801, in Hamburg and in Denmark. The Oeconomische Encyclopädie of 1842 calls it “ein im Oesterreichischen sehr beliebtes Kartenspiel … aus dem bekannten Piquet und dem veralteten Mariage zusammengesetzt” — assembled out of Piquet and Mariage — and that is the shape of it: the trick play is Mariage’s, the combinations and their scoring are Piquet’s.

The ruleset here is the last one codified for the Austro-Hungarian market: S. Ulmann, Das Buch der Familienspiele, A. Hartleben, Wien/München/Pest 1890 — the edition that called the game Franzefuß. The nine-card deal is its newer form; eight cards is the older one. The 1829 Vienna rulebook and the many that copied its wording omit the value of the combinations altogether, so those come from Piquet, exactly as the encyclopedia instructs: “die Berechnung der Sequenzen, Stiche und Punktezahl, bis zu der man die ganze Partie spielt, ist genau wie beim Pikett”. Terze 3, Quarte 4, Quinte 15, four of a kind 14, three 3, and a partie to a hundred.

Honestly: the game barely outlived the century that made it. It survived as Tärtele in Alsace until the second war, and lives on as Austrian Tartl and Hungarian Tartli. If you were dealt a hand of this in Prague in 1910 you would have been playing something already old-fashioned — and you would have held a German-suited pack, not this one.
