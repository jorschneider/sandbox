# Ten Books for America's 250th

*A July 4th reading list, drawn from the ChinaTalk review archive.*

This one is the big birthday — 250 years since the Declaration. I spend most of my
reading life on the CCP, the Eastern Front, and semiconductor supply chains, but the
Americana shelf keeps pulling me back. Here are ten books I've read and loved that,
taken together, run the story from the colonial seedbed to the man who ran the
United States Senate. Read them in order and you get a crash course in how America
actually got built — and why the machine still (mostly) works.

---

## 1. Albion's Seed — David Hackett Fischer (1989)

**The origins.** Four British folkways — Puritans, Cavaliers, Quakers, and Borderers —
that still map onto American politics four centuries later. The best single answer to
"why is America like this?" and the right place to start any 250th-anniversary syllabus.
If you liked how Henrich's *The Secret of Our Success* used culture as a causal engine,
this is that method applied to one country.

## 2. The British Are Coming — Rick Atkinson (2019)

**1775–1777.** The war itself, from Lexington to Princeton. Atkinson doesn't quite hit
Toll/Catton altitude — the sourcing just isn't as rich for the 18th century — but this
is still the best modern operational history of the Revolution, and Washington's retreat
across New Jersey remains the most improbable episode in American military history.
*Pair with:* Alan Taylor's *American Revolutions* for the revisionist, continental view
of the same events — the Revolution as civil war and imperial land grab, not just
Whig triumph.

## 3. Six Frigates — Ian Toll (2006)

**The early republic.** Toll is so good I stopped reading other early-navy histories.
Ostensibly a book about warships; actually a book about state capacity — how a broke
new republic argued itself into funding a navy, built an industrial base from scratch,
and staffed it with maniacs. The 1790s shipbuilding debates read like a CHIPS Act
markup. The USS Constitution is still in the water; read this before you visit her.

## 4. Empire of the Summer Moon — S.C. Gwynne (2010)

**The frontier.** Quanah Parker and the forty-year war between the Comanche empire and
the United States. Gwynne writes violence the way Stahel writes logistics — grounded,
unsentimental, gripping. The continental conquest is the part of the American story the
July 4th genre usually skips; it shouldn't.

## 5. Glory Road — Bruce Catton (1952)

**1862–1863.** The middle volume of the Army of the Potomac trilogy: Fredericksburg,
Chancellorsville, and the roads that converge on a Pennsylvania crossroads town.
Catton's citizen-soldiers absorb the worst run of generalship any American army has
ever suffered and somehow hold together long enough to find themselves in the first
three days of July 1863 — with word of Vicksburg's surrender arriving on the Fourth.
Nobody writes the texture of an army — the mud, the rumor mill, the morale — like
Catton. The most July 4th book on this list, and it never mentions the holiday.

## 6. U.S. Grant and the American Military Tradition — Bruce Catton (1954)

**The second founding.** Made my top ten last year. Catton compresses everything —
Grant the failure, Grant the general, Grant the president — into 200 perfect pages, and
makes the case that the citizen-soldier who grinds is the truest American military type.
*Pair with:* Goodwin's *Team of Rivals* for Lincoln, and Murray & Hsieh's *A Savage War*
for the operational view of the war that re-founded the country.

## 7. Only Yesterday — Frederick Lewis Allen (1931)

**The 1920s.** An informal history of the decade, written in 1931 while the wreckage
was still smoking. Allen invented the instant-history genre and nobody has done it
better since — flappers, Ponzi, the Big Bull Market, all with the pacing of a novel.
*Pair with:* *Bubble in the Sun* on the Florida land boom, the purest distillation of
American real-estate mania ever printed.

## 8. With the Old Breed — E.B. Sledge (1981)

**WWII.** Peleliu and Okinawa through the eyes of a mortarman from Alabama. A horrific
book written with compassion and humanity — the citizen-soldier tradition Catton traced
to Appomattox, picked up eighty years later in the coral mud of the Pacific. The cost
side of the ledger for everything else on this list.
*Pair with:* Ian Toll's Pacific War trilogy, a modern masterpiece and the best account
of how America actually fought and won a global war.

## 9. Command and Control — Eric Schlosser (2013)

**The Cold War.** Horrifying. The Damascus Titan II accident interleaved with the
whole secret history of America nearly nuking itself — including the week of JFK's
inauguration, when the Air Force dropped a hydrogen bomb over North Carolina and
three of four safety switches failed. The price of the American century, itemized.

## 10. Master of the Senate — Robert Caro (2002)

**How power actually works.** Caro opens with a hundred-page history of the Senate
itself — an institution purpose-built to stop things — then shows how Lyndon Johnson
made it move, culminating in the Civil Rights Act of 1957. Torigian gave us the
anatomy of power in the CCP; Caro is the American equivalent, and volume three is the
summit of the project. If the Fourth is about self-government, this is the machinery,
warts and all.
*Pair with:* *The Power Broker*, obviously, and Beverly Gage's *G-Man*, the other
great study of unaccountable American power.

## On the nightstand for the next 250

Books I haven't gotten to yet but that this list argues for:

- **The Fate of the Day** — Rick Atkinson (2025). Volume two: Saratoga, Valley Forge,
  the French alliance. The reviews say the trilogy levels up here.
- **The Radicalism of the American Revolution** — Gordon S. Wood (1992). Wood died
  last month at 92; the tributes sent me back to the masterwork I never read. His
  argument — that the Revolution was the most radical social transformation in
  history precisely because it looked so moderate — deserves a proper reckoning.
- **The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution** — Bernard Bailyn (1967).
  Nathans' samizdat chapters were my favorite reading of last year; Bailyn did the
  same trick for revolutionary pamphlets. Thomas Paine would have killed it on
  Substack.
- **Battle Cry of Freedom** — James McPherson (1988). I've read Catton, *A Savage
  War*, *Attack and Die*, and *For Cause and Comrades*; time to finally read the
  synthesis everyone measures against.
- **Frederick Douglass: Prophet of Freedom** — David Blight (2018). "What to the
  Slave Is the Fourth of July?" is the sharpest thing ever said about this holiday,
  and Douglass is America's great dissident — Solzhenitsyn with better speeches.
