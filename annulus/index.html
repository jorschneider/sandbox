Part I

The stage: an annulus

A conformal field theory (CFT) is a quantum field theory with no built-in sense of scale: zoom in, zoom out, bend the geometry smoothly — the physics only cares about angles. In two dimensions this is an absurdly powerful constraint, because the symmetry algebra (the Virasoro algebra, with its famous central charge \(c\)) is infinite-dimensional. Whole families of 2d CFTs can be solved exactly. They describe statistical systems exactly at a critical point — the Ising magnet at its Curie temperature (\(c=\tfrac12\)) — and they are the worldsheet theories of string theory.

But real systems end. A magnet has edges; an open string has endpoints. Boundary CFT, founded by John Cardy in the late 1980s, asks: which boundary conditions can a CFT be given without ruining its conformal symmetry? The cleanest laboratory for that question is the surface in this thesis’s title.

The annulus — a disc with a hole, \(q\le|z|\le1\) — is the unique surface with two boundary circles and exactly one conformal “shape” parameter, the modulus \(q\). Under the conformal map

$$w=\log z:\qquad \{\,q\le|z|\le1\,\}\;\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\;\text{a cylinder of circumference }2\pi\text{ and length }\log\tfrac1q,$$

it unrolls into a finite cylinder: one independent conformal field theory, pinched between two boundary conditions, call them α on one edge and β on the other. The figure below plays the map on a loop — the radial direction of the annulus becomes the length of the cylinder. Drag to orbit.

Part II

One surface, two stories

Here is the engine of the whole subject. The partition function of the CFT on the annulus, \(Z_{\alpha\beta}\), can be computed in two completely different ways, depending on which direction around the cylinder you call time.

Open-string channel. Let time run around the cylinder. Then you are watching an open string (a segment) with one endpoint glued to boundary condition α and the other to β, going once around a loop. The partition function simply counts its quantum states.

Closed-string channel. Let time run along the cylinder. Now the boundaries are not where strings end — they are initial and final states. A closed string (a loop) is emitted by the state \(\langle\alpha|\), propagates the length of the cylinder, and is absorbed by \(|\beta\rangle\). The boundary condition has become a vector in the closed-string Hilbert space: a boundary state. Toggle the channels below.

Open-string channel

$$Z_{\alpha\beta}(q)\;=\;\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal H_{\alpha\beta}}\,q^{\,L_0-\frac{c}{24}}\;=\;\sum_i n_{\alpha\beta}^{\;i}\,\chi_i(q)$$

A trace over the open-string Hilbert space. The \(n_{\alpha\beta}^{\;i}\) count how many times each representation \(i\) of the symmetry appears — so they must be non-negative integers.

Closed-string channel

$$Z_{\alpha\beta}\;=\;\langle\alpha|\;\tilde q^{\,\frac12\left(L_0+\bar L_0-\frac{c}{12}\right)}\;|\beta\rangle, \qquad \tilde q = e^{-2\pi i/\tau},\; q = e^{2\pi i\tau}$$

A quantum-mechanical amplitude between two boundary states. The two channels are related by the modular transformation \(\tau\mapsto-1/\tau\), under which characters mix: \(\chi_i(q)=\sum_j S_{ij}\,\chi_j(\tilde q)\).

Demanding that both stories describe the same number is a ferociously restrictive consistency condition. In string theory it is how D-branes announce themselves; in statistical mechanics it classifies the boundary conditions of critical systems. It is, almost certainly, the spine of this thesis.

Part III

Boundary states: the perfect mirror

What kind of vector can \(|\beta\rangle\) be? Conformal symmetry at the boundary means no energy flows across it: every left-moving excitation must reflect into a right-moving one with the same profile. As an operator statement on the state,

$$\big(L_n-\bar L_{-n}\big)\,|B\rangle \;=\; 0\qquad\text{for all }n .$$

Ishibashi (1989): within each irreducible representation \(j\) of the symmetry algebra there is exactly one solution — a maximally entangled, mirror-symmetric pairing of left- and right-movers:

$$|j\rangle\!\rangle \;=\; \sum_{N}\,|j,N\rangle\otimes\overline{|j,N\rangle}.$$

But generic linear combinations of Ishibashi states are not physical boundary conditions. Cardy (1989) imposed the channel duality of Part II and found the allowed combinations are quantized: there is one consistent boundary state per primary field, with coefficients fixed by the modular S-matrix,

$$|\tilde a\rangle \;=\; \sum_j \frac{S_{aj}}{\sqrt{S_{0j}}}\;|j\rangle\!\rangle, \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad n_{ab}^{\;i} \;=\; \sum_j \frac{S_{aj}\,S_{bj}\,S_{ij}}{S_{0j}} \;=\; N_{ab}^{\;i}\;\in\;\mathbb Z_{\ge0},$$

the Verlinde fusion numbers — integers, as the open channel demands. This is one of the great consistency miracles of 2d physics. For the Ising model it hands you exactly three conformal boundary conditions, and they are precisely the physical ones: spins frozen up, frozen down, or left free:

$$|\widetilde{+}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,|\mathbf 1\rangle\!\rangle+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,|\epsilon\rangle\!\rangle+2^{-1/4}\,|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle,\qquad |\widetilde{-}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,|\mathbf 1\rangle\!\rangle+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,|\epsilon\rangle\!\rangle-2^{-1/4}\,|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle,\qquad |\widetilde{f}\rangle=|\mathbf 1\rangle\!\rangle-|\epsilon\rangle\!\rangle .$$

The figure shows a boundary circle vibrating in its Ishibashi modes. The two faint curves are the left- and right-moving halves; the bright curve is their sum — a standing wave, because the boundary reflects perfectly. Switch between pure Ishibashi states and the three physical Cardy states of the Ising model.

One more character who will matter later: the overlap of a boundary state with the vacuum, \(g_a=\langle 0|\tilde a\rangle = S_{a0}/\sqrt{S_{00}}\) — the Affleck–Ludwig ground-state degeneracy. Its logarithm is a boundary analogue of the central charge, and it counts down monotonically along boundary renormalization-group flows (the g-theorem). Keep an eye on it.

Part IV · imagined from here to the end of the section

The thesis, reimagined

So what did Roper actually do for four years? We can’t know — but the title plus the supervisor make the shape of it easy to guess. By 2005, Cardy’s rational story (Parts I–III) was twenty years old and textbook material. The live problem in Wheater’s group was what happens when the tidy assumptions fail — specifically in logarithmic CFTs like the \(c=-2\) triplet model, where representations refuse to decompose, the Verlinde formula breaks, and nobody could fully agree on what the right set of boundary states even was. Kawai & Wheater had proposed one set using symplectic fermions (2001); Gaberdiel, Kausch, Bredthauer and Flohr had competing constructions; the annulus amplitudes were the battleground on which the proposals were judged. A DPhil titled “Boundary States in Conformal Field Theories of the Annulus”, in that group, in those years, almost writes its own table of contents:

Chapter 1

Conformal field theory in two dimensions

Virasoro algebra, primaries and descendants, characters, minimal models, modular transformations. The standard sixty-page throat-clearing of every worldsheet thesis of the era.

Chapter 2

Boundary CFT and the annulus

Cardy’s gluing condition, Ishibashi states, the annulus amplitude in both channels, the Cardy condition and the Verlinde formula. Worked examples: Ising boundary states; Dirichlet and Neumann branes of the free boson, with T-duality exchanging them.

Chapter 3

Free-field boundary states

Symplectic fermions as a free-field laboratory: explicit coherent-state (“squeezed state”) boundary states, cylinder amplitudes computed head-on, and the first warning signs — zero modes and logarithms creeping into the characters.

Chapter 4 · the heart of it

Boundary states in the c = −2 triplet model

The indecomposable representations of \(\mathcal W(2,3,3,3)\); why Verlinde fails; the candidate boundary-state sets (Kawai–Wheater vs. Gaberdiel–Kausch) compared by demanding that every annulus amplitude expand in characters with sensible non-negative integer coefficients — the rational-CFT consistency test, wielded as a weapon in a logarithmic theory.

Chapter 5

Annulus amplitudes, τ-dependence and the failure of factorization

The logarithmic surprise: modular transforms of the triplet characters generate explicit \(\tau\)-dependent coefficients, so the “S-matrix” isn’t a matrix of numbers anymore. What survives of Cardy’s construction, what must be replaced, and which open-string spectra are physically acceptable.

Chapter 6

Conclusions and outlook

A consistent (if incomplete) catalogue of annulus partition functions for the logarithmic theory, a conjecture about which boundary states are fundamental, and the customary final paragraph hoping someone will sort out the general logarithmic case. (Spoiler for Part V: someone did.)

Why this guess is defensible: the title’s odd phrasing — CFTs of the annulus, plural — suggests a survey across theories of the one geometry, exactly what a compare-the-boundary-state-proposals thesis would be. And every Wheater student of that era (Kawai before him, Atkin after) worked within arm’s reach of the \(c=-2\) problem. But to be clear, one more time: the real Chapter 4 could be something else entirely, and only a trip to the Bodleian stacks would settle it.

Part V · back to verified history

What happened to the field since 2009

Boundary states turned out to be one of the best investments in theoretical physics. A concept built for critical magnets and D-branes in the 1980s has spent the years since 2009 colonizing quantum information, condensed matter, and the black-hole information problem. The short version, in roughly chronological order:

  1. 2009–2013

    Logarithmic boundary CFT grows up

    The problem at the (imagined) heart of the thesis got properly solved: Gaberdiel, Runkel & Wood constructed the full consistent boundary theory of the \(c=-2\) triplet model, indecomposable modules and all, and Cardy himself weighed in on logarithmic CFT as the continuum theory of disordered systems. The annulus amplitudes that were once controversial are now textbook exercises in a (still growing) logarithmic literature.

  2. 2011

    Boundary states go holographic: AdS/BCFT

    Takayanagi (building on Karch–Randall) proposed the gravitational dual of a boundary CFT: the boundary extends into the higher-dimensional bulk as an end-of-the-world brane. The Affleck–Ludwig \(g\)-factor of Part III becomes the tension/angle of the brane. The figure below shows the cartoon every talk in this subject now opens with.

  3. 2006 → 2010s

    Quantum quenches: boundary states as initial conditions for everything

    Calabrese & Cardy’s insight — that a system suddenly yanked away from equilibrium is well modelled by a regularized boundary state, \(|\psi_0\rangle\sim e^{-\tau_0 H}|B\rangle\) — became the standard analytic handle on thermalization just as cold-atom experiments made quenches routine. Light-cone spreading of entanglement, generalized Gibbs ensembles: boundary states underneath all of it.

  4. 2012–present

    The bootstrap reaches the boundary

    The numerical conformal bootstrap revolution was extended to boundaries and defects (Liendo, Rastelli & van Rees), turning boundary CFT data in higher dimensions — once thought hopeless — into something you can corner with crossing equations on a laptop.

  5. 2016–2017

    The g-theorem becomes quantum information

    Friedan–Konechny’s g-theorem got an entropic proof (Casini, Landea & Torroba): the boundary entropy \(\ln g\) decreases under boundary RG flow as a consequence of strong subadditivity of entanglement entropy. Meanwhile Cardy showed bulk RG flows themselves can be steered using boundary states as variational tools.

  6. 2016–present

    Boundary states classify phases of matter

    In condensed matter, whether a symmetric, gapped boundary state exists at all became a diagnostic for symmetry-protected topological phases and anomalies (Cho, Ludwig & Ryu and many after): an anomalous edge is precisely a CFT that admits no symmetric boundary state. Cardy states also became entanglement-spectrum fingerprints of SPT phases.

  7. 2018–present

    Topological defect lines and non-invertible symmetry

    The modern generalized-symmetry program — arguably the hottest topic in formal QFT — runs on topological defect lines acting on boundary states (Chang, Lin, Shao, Wang & Yin). Cardy’s annulus consistency conditions reappear, upgraded, as the axioms of fusion-category symmetry and the “SymTFT” sandwich, where a physical theory is a choice of boundary state of a topological bulk.

  8. 2019–2021

    Black holes: islands and end-of-the-world branes

    The breakthrough Page-curve computations of the black-hole information paradox (Penington; Penington, Shenker, Stanford & Yang; Almheiri et al.) model black-hole microstates with end-of-the-world branes — boundary states again — and the “island formula” was sharpened in doubly-holographic AdS/BCFT setups directly descended from the 2011 construction.

  9. 2019–present

    Measurement-induced phases

    The newest arrival: monitored quantum circuits undergo entanglement phase transitions whose critical points are described by (often logarithmic!) boundary conformal field theory — the \(c=-2\)-style machinery of the imagined Chapter 4, reborn in quantum computing.

Epilogue

The author’s own RG flow

And the author? Will Roper submitted the thesis, left the worldsheet, and underwent the most dramatic boundary flow in the story. He became chief architect at the Missile Defense Agency; founding director of the Pentagon’s Strategic Capabilities Office (2012); then Assistant Secretary of the Air Force for Acquisition, Technology & Logistics (2018–2021), where he championed digital engineering, launched the Skyborg autonomy program, and revealed that the Next Generation Air Dominance program had secretly flown a full-scale demonstrator. He then founded Istari Digital, a company whose pitch — aircraft assembled and certified from validated digital models before metal is ever bent — is, if you squint, channel duality for airplanes: the same object, computed in a different representation.

The thesis itself still sits, unscanned, in the Oxford stacks. If anyone reading this has Bodleian access: the physics community (population: this webpage) awaits Chapter 4.
