What is a certificate of insurance (COI)?

RequirementsUpdated January 2026~4 min read

Quick answer

A certificate of insurance (COI) is a one-page document from your insurer that proves your business has active coverage. It summarizes your policies, coverage types, limits, and dates — without exposing the full policy.

Clients, landlords, and vendors commonly require a COI before you can sign a contract, lease space, or start a job. It's how the other side confirms you're actually insured.

What's on a certificate of insurance

Most COIs use a standard ACORD form. At a glance, it shows:

  • The insured — your business name and address.
  • The insurer(s) — the carrier(s) providing coverage.
  • Coverage types — e.g. general liability, commercial auto, workers' comp.
  • Limits — the maximum the policy will pay (e.g. $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate).
  • Policy numbers and effective/expiration dates.
  • The certificate holder — the party you're giving it to.
  • Additional insured / endorsements — if others are named on your coverage.
A COI is evidence of coverage — it doesn't grant or change coverage by itself. The actual policy controls what's covered.

What is an "additional insured"?

This is the part that trips people up. An additional insured is a person or business added to your policy so they're also protected by your coverage for claims arising from your work.

Example: a general contractor requires a subcontractor to name them as an additional insured. If the sub causes damage, the GC can be protected under the sub's policy. Two things to know:

  • Being listed as a certificate holder is not the same as being an additional insured. The first just receives the certificate; the second is actually covered.
  • Additional insured status requires an endorsement to the policy — not just a line on the certificate.

How to get a certificate of insurance

  1. Ask your insurer or broker for a COI (usually free).
  2. Provide the exact name and address of the party requesting it (the certificate holder).
  3. Share any specific requirements — minimum limits, additional insured, waiver of subrogation, etc.
  4. Most carriers or brokers issue it within minutes to a day.

If a client's requirements exceed your current limits, you may need to raise them before the certificate can be issued as requested.
