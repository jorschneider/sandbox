Can Chinese models take Taiwan?

We put frontier and Chinese open models in command of a 2028 invasion — and scored how far each one got.

In the CSIS Taiwan Operational Wargame, each model commands both sides — RED · China vs BLUE · US / Japan / Taiwan — while a deterministic, rules-faithful engine adjudicates every move. The headline number is offense: how much of Taiwan a model seizes when it runs the PLA. The Western frontier — Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT‑5.5 — faces the most advanced Chinese models — DeepSeek, Qwen, GLM, MiniMax, Kimi — and each takes its turn as the invader.

A research prototype built on the published CSIS wargame “The First Battle of the Next War.”

The gap

Models ace isolated tasks. Can they run a campaign?

Benchmarks mostly measure execution: answer this question, write this function, pass this exam. Real command is different — a sequence of interdependent decisions under uncertainty, against an adversary who is adapting to you, where the cost of a mistake compounds over days. WarBench puts two models on opposite sides of a serious operational wargame and asks: who manages escalation, allocates scarce forces, and reads the opponent better?

The wargame is CSIS’s Taiwan Operational Wargame — a 2028 Chinese invasion of Taiwan, the model from the report The First Battle of the Next War. Crucially, its rules state that combat is resolved by “objective criteria … computer programs, Excel spreadsheets, and lookup tables with die rolls,” not by a human umpire’s judgment. So we make the adjudicator deterministic code — the models are the players, and the engine resolves their orders exactly as the umpire would.

Why that matters. A coded referee makes the eval reproducible (same orders + seed → same game), gives an objective winner from the rulebook’s victory conditions, and removes the bias and noise of an LLM judge. The only judgment calls in a game are the two commanders’ orders.

How it works

Two commanders, eleven phases, a coded umpire

Each turn is 3.5 days; games run six to eight turns — the intense opening weeks of the campaign. Every turn, the engine shows each side what it can see (fog of war from an ISR phase) and the legal orders for the current phase. Each model returns structured orders — how many missile salvos at which targets, how many sorties to CAP vs. anti-ship strike, how many amphibious flotillas to commit, ground posture — and the engine resolves them.

The decision-bearing phases of the CSIS cycle of play. Illegal or impossible orders are clamped and recorded — a commander that can’t issue valid orders is itself a signal.

The outcome is computed, not voted on

Victory is scored on the rulebook’s spectrum — the prospect for Taiwan’s continuity — from Chinese victory through stalemate to Chinese defeat, read directly off the game state: how much PLA lodgment is ashore, how many functional ports and airfields China holds, and how badly the amphibious fleet was attrited.

Fidelity

Grounded in the actual CSIS tables

The engine’s combat is not hand-waved. The core calculators are ported from the CSIS workbooks and rulebook tables, each checked against the source by “golden” tests that reproduce the workbook’s own cached outputs:

  • Casualty calculator — per-side personnel losses & KIA (exact, all four nations).
  • Amphibious lift — the exact 60 × afloat / 36 formula with the turn-1 surge.
  • Air combat — the real per-airframe Quality constants (J-20/F-35/F-22 = 8, …).
  • Ground combat CRT — terrain modifiers, odds bands, step losses, FEBA movement (worked example reproduced).
  • Airbase missile attack — SAM intercept on 1–18, hardened-shelter kills on 1–15, open on 1–17.
  • Anti-ship strike — rules Table 5H: each leaking missile rolls a d20 for ship hits; a natural 20 destroys the flotilla.

A 30 km hex ground game (with Taiwan’s real Table 10A order of battle) and scenario excursions (delayed US entry, a neutral Japan) are also implemented. Two minor calculators remain representative and are flagged as such.

Scoring

Offense is the headline

The CSIS historical base case is strongly defender-favored — the coalition almost always repels the invasion, which is itself the report’s finding. But that makes a poor benchmark: if everyone loses as RED, nothing separates the models. So the featured run uses a balanced scenario — a maximal PLA sealift and a one-turn lag in US entry — calibrated so the invasion is genuinely winnable and the outcome depends on how well each side is played. (The historical case is still on the board, for honesty.) Each model commands both sides, so WarBench reports:

  • Offense — the headline: how much of Taiwan a model takes as RED (a continuous score from lodgment, force ratio, fleet survival). Higher is a better invader.
  • Defense — how little it concedes as BLUE. Lower is a better defender.
  • Outcome & Elo — head-to-head on the victory spectrum, sides swapped to cancel side advantage.
  • Reliability — how often a model failed to produce a legal, parseable order and fell back to a scripted baseline. Lower is better.

Games where the live API rate-limited so badly that a side mostly fell back to the baseline are flagged degraded and excluded from the rankings — otherwise a throttled game silently becomes script-vs-script.

Reproduce

It’s all code

The engine, the faithful calculator ports with golden tests, the hex ground game, the model commanders, and the tournament harness are open. Heuristic (no-API) commanders let the whole pipeline — and the test suite — run with zero network access.

pip install -e ".[claude]" · pytest -q · python analysis/run_real_tournament.py · python analysis/build_site.py

WarBench is a research prototype and an adaptation of the CSIS Taiwan Operational Wargame (Cancian, Cancian & Heginbotham), not an official CSIS product. Scenarios depict a hypothetical conflict for analytic purposes. Built with the wargame-eval harness.
